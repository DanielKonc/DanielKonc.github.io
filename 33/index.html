Karakert — The White Jewel of Aparan, The Flower on Sariba's Grave, The Dreamer's Death, or The City of a Thousand Temples. These and many others are names given to the great city sitting astride the main crossroads of ancient trade routes. The constant flow of trade feeds it, making it swell in every direction so quickly that the masons can barely keep up with the walls. As a result, it is a mishmash of styles and eras, surrounded by something like three successive rings of fortifications as generations of rulers tried, entirely without success, to protect their investments from raids and attacks. But the city's bustling growth always outpaced its builders, and so at least half its houses now stand beyond the walls — not merely slums, but craftsmen's and merchants' districts as well. The city has been sacked many times throughout its history, but the preceding period of stability emboldened the enterprising to construct permanent stone buildings outside the protection of its high walls. Now, however, with the kingdom of Sariba in decline, who knows what will happen — or who will march against whom with fire and sword.

Beyond the city limits lie vast stretches of farmland, growing crops and raising livestock to feed Karakert's 70,000 permanent residents, not to mention at least another 20,000 pilgrims and merchants passing through or staying only briefly. The forests were cut down long ago, making timber both scarce and expensive. Which is particularly unfortunate given that the Aparan is a broad, deep, navigable river and the city has always maintained a strong naval presence. But the senseless plundering of natural resources has reached the point where only a handful of the city's wealthiest individuals can afford a ship.

The streets are filled with noise and commotion, people jostling one another along roads far too narrow for the crowds. Stalls occupy every square, with merchants doing their best to sell their wares. At any given time there are at least five different markets drawing people from far and wide, as certain commodities are available only when a particular caravan arrives.

As one of the city's names suggests, people come here from all across the kingdom and beyond. Dreamers looking for a better life, greater challenges, an end to hardship and trouble. And many lose their illusions within the first few days in their new home. Like a carnivorous plant luring a fly with the promise of something delicious only to devour it, Karakert, cheerful and colourful on the outside, mercilessly crushes anyone who fails to keep their wits about them. That is the main reason there are so many beggars here, stretching out their hands in desperate appeal to anyone willing to listen, even for a moment. And consequently, it is also why every imaginable religion and cult thrives here, from the familiar to the utterly obscure ones you've never heard of in your life.

It stands to reason that a city like this has crime — organised and well hidden. The underworld is well fed and wealthier than anyone could ever hope to map. Layers upon layers of groups, gangs, networks and associations, probably extending deeper than anyone can see. If Karakert's criminal underworld has someone at the very top, that fact is not common knowledge even among its own members.

Architecturally, the city is as diverse as it could possibly be. Houses hundreds of years old — the city's true history has been lost in the mists of time, although a few years ago it celebrated its four-hundredth anniversary, an arbitrary figure derived from the oldest surviving records — stand side by side with new construction. The locals demolish and rebuild even within the walls, and every now and then the city gets conquered and burned, generally with the districts outside the protection of the fortifications taking the worst of it. Yet the first things to rise again are always the spires and towers of churches and temples, because just as bread satisfies people's hunger for food, religion satisfies their hunger for nourishment of the soul.

Beyond the first wall — known as the Black Blackness, because it is coated in a layer of asphalt and is said never to have been breached — stand several genuinely ancient buildings that, at first glance, are clearly more than four hundred years old. Some are empty, others inhabited, but either way everyone gives them a respectful distance and whispers about ghosts, spirits and curses. About both the houses and their tenants. The only ancient building open to the public is Brytus Keep, the ruler's seat from the days when Karakert was the capital of an empire that has long since vanished. Today it houses the city council, the captain of the guard and the advisory assembly.

During the day.

At night, the building stands empty, because strange things are said to happen within its walls, just as they do in the others.

There are, of course, plenty of laws, but most are nothing unusual by the standards of a large city. Slavery and the ownership of humanoid beings are forbidden, spellcasting within the city walls is heavily restricted, and every spellcaster must register immediately upon entering the city. The council has recently invested heavily in detecting illegal magic and unregistered spellcasters — which is why even the underworld has gone back to solving its problems with the good old-fashioned cloak and dagger.

Karakert has a lower sales tax than Sariba and the surrounding kingdoms, but merchants who fail to sell their goods here must pay a fairly steep transit toll. The council introduced the system to encourage caravans to trade here rather than continue farther along the route with their cargo. And the plan worked exactly as intended: very few merchants now carry their goods onward, and the city has effectively forced contracts to be concluded locally that would otherwise have been made in the capital. Could this have something to do with the decline? Who knows, eh…

And officially, any religion may be practised here, provided it does not restrict anyone else's freedom of worship. Incidents do occur from time to time, but the penalties are severe enough that people tend to watch themselves.

There is, of course, much more that could be written, but who has time to describe every subtle nuance and eccentricity? The professional but hopelessly overworked guardians of law and order, for example. The University of Experimental Astrochemistry. The gas used to light some parts of the city. The endless and brutally bloody struggle of the Green Terrorists. The hideous and monstrous creatures lurking in the Aparan…
